Attack types

What attackers do

🎣
QR Phishing

Fake QR codes that redirect to credential-harvesting sites designed to mimic banks, carriers, and delivery services. Often spread via email or printed flyers.

High risk
💳
Payment Fraud

Sticker QR codes placed over legitimate ones on parking meters, restaurant tables, and charity boxes — redirecting payments directly to fraudsters.

High risk
📦
Fake Delivery Scams

"Scan to reschedule your delivery" notices that harvest personal data or silently install tracking software. Common in Japan and across Asia.

Medium risk
🔗
Redirect Chains

URL shorteners and multi-hop redirects that hide the real destination — impossible to evaluate without live AI inspection of the final URL.

Medium risk
📧
Email Filter Bypass

QR code images embedded in emails bypass text-based spam filters. Now the #1 method used to deliver phishing to corporate inboxes undetected.

Emerging
💻
Malware Droppers

QR codes that trigger app installs or browser exploits — silently installing ransomware or keyloggers without any user interaction beyond the scan.

Emerging
Five red
flags to spot
before scanning.

Even without an AI tool, these signals are worth knowing.

Scan a QR code now
01

Sticker over the code

Touch the QR code. A sticker on top of a printed QR is a near-certain sign of tampering.

02

Misspelled domain

paypa1.com, amaz0n-secure.net — character substitutions that look right at a glance.

03

URL shortener

bit.ly, tinyurl — the final destination is completely hidden until you land there.

04

Immediate login request

Legitimate services rarely demand credentials the moment you arrive from a QR scan.

05

Urgency language

"Act now or your account closes." Real companies don't deliver ultimatums via QR code.
