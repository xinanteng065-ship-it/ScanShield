The threat landscape

QR scams are on the rise

QR phishing — or "quishing" — is now one of the fastest-growing cybersecurity attack methods worldwide.

Attack types

What attackers do

🎣
QR Phishing

Fake QR codes that redirect to credential-harvesting sites designed to look like banks, carriers, and delivery services.

High risk
💳
Payment Fraud

Sticker QR codes placed over real ones on parking meters and restaurant tables — redirecting payments to fraudsters.

High risk
📦
Fake Delivery Scams

"Scan to reschedule delivery" notices that collect personal data or install tracking software. Very common in Japan.

Medium risk
🔗
Redirect Chains

URL shorteners that hide the real destination — impossible to evaluate without live AI inspection of the final URL.

Medium risk
📧
Email Filter Bypass

QR images in emails bypass text-based spam filters, landing phishing payloads directly in corporate inboxes.

Emerging
💻
Malware Droppers

QR codes that trigger browser exploits or app installs — silently deploying ransomware or keyloggers.

Emerging
Five red flags
to spot before scanning.

Even without a tool, these signals are worth knowing.

Scan a QR code now
01

Sticker over the code

Touch the QR. A sticker on top of a printed code is a near-certain sign of tampering.

02

Misspelled domain

paypa1.com, amaz0n-secure.net — character substitutions that look right at a glance.

03

URL shortener

bit.ly, tinyurl — the final destination is completely hidden until you land there.

04

Immediate login request

Legitimate services rarely demand credentials the moment you arrive from a QR scan.

05

Urgency language

"Act now or your account closes." Real companies don't deliver ultimatums via QR code.
